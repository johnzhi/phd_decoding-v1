## Historical country classification and proposed liability

The article assumes that fossil point-source CCS is accounted for as an emission reduction at the capture location. It explicitly calls the classification a political question still unresolved at the time and treats biomass combustion separately. This cannot establish a universal classification of atmospheric removal or biomass CCS.

Tables 2 and 3 distinguish the following historical cases:

| Case | Capture | Storage | Mechanism in the assumed framework | Liability arrangement discussed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Annex I | Same Annex I country | Annex I mitigation | Storage-country inventory |
| 2 | Annex I | Another Annex I country | Annex I mitigation | Storage-country inventory |
| 3 | Non-Annex I | Annex I country | CDM | Permanent CER; storage-country inventory |
| 4 | Annex I | Non-Annex I country | Annex I mitigation | Proposed capture-country inventory responsibility |
| 5 | Non-Annex I | Same non-Annex I country | CDM | Proposed temporary-credit buyer liability |
| 6 | Non-Annex I | Another non-Annex I country | CDM | Proposed temporary-credit buyer liability |

The six-case reconstruction corroborates the printed classification. Non-Annex I participation alone does not imply CDM: case 4 is the counterexample within the authors' framework. A third-country buyer can participate without becoming the physical capture or storage country. Ratification, inventory quality and the distinction between an inventory obligation and an operator's liability matter.

These are historical arguments and proposed arrangements. The governing instruments and later amendments have not been checked here. They do not establish current project eligibility, permits, enforcement or an enacted cross-border liability allocation. The authors explicitly leave multi-exporter stores, stock-dependent releases and transboundary reservoirs as complications; the simple table does not solve those cases.

## Resource and cost accounting

All 30 lower/upper interval cells in Table 1 were transcribed. Their regional components close, and summing the two total rows gives the quoted **476–5,880 GtCO₂** range. The five columns cover remaining/depleted oil, remaining/depleted gas and enhanced coal-bed methane. Saline aquifers are discussed in prose but have no explicit table column. The reported **1,660 Gt best estimate** cannot be derived from those bounds alone. Their arithmetic midpoint is 3,178 Gt, which is neither a correction to the best estimate nor an identified probability mean. The table's Annex I allocation is an author calculation from Hendriks et al. (2004), including a caveat about the former Soviet Union. Comparing paired lower or upper totals does not prove that non-Annex resources dominate every possible joint interval combination. None of the historical stocks supplies an annual pressure-constrained injection limit.

The printed cost identity is:

`C_CCS = C_capture + C_transportation + C_storage + C_monitoring`.

The components must share currency/year, service scope and mass denominator. Capture costs of 24–52 €/t avoided, pipeline costs of 1–6 €/t per 100 km, storage costs of 1–11 €/t and EOR costs of −10 to 30 €/t are historical, differently qualified literature ranges. The full −3 to 106 €/t avoided range cannot be reconstructed without the capture variant, distance, monitoring cost and common denominator. This is missing case information, rather than proof of an arithmetic error. EOR revenue reduces monetary cost; it is not an avoided-emissions credit.

Its `CO2e` label means CO₂ emitted from energy use; it is not a quantified complete multi-gas CO₂-equivalent inventory. ## The financial table is preserved and unreproduced

The second printed identity is:

`V_temp = V_perm − RC`.

Here `RC` is the discounted purchase of permanent credits to compensate for later releases. These are monetary values. The paper assumes a constant permanent-credit price for Table 4:

| Financial discount rate | Release 0% | Release 0.01% | Release 0.1% | Release 1% |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1% | 100 | 98.8 | 90.6 | 48.5 |
| 5% | 100 | 99.6 | 97.7 | 80.8 |
| 10% | 100 | 99.7 | 98.7 | 88.0 |

Cells are percentages of permanent-credit value. They increase with financial discounting and decrease with the stated release rate. The complete replacement-cost formula, release-stock basis, payment timing, verification/renewal schedule and calculation horizon are not supplied.

An explicitly analyst model treats one initial cohort as losing fraction `lambda` of its remaining stock each year and pays replacement at year end. With a constant price, its unrestricted value fraction is `r/(r + lambda)`. Advancing each replacement by a year provides a second timing comparison. Neither convention reproduces the nine nonzero-release cells within their displayed 0.1 percentage-point precision. For 1% release and 1% discounting, they give 50% and 49.5%, versus the source's 48.5%.

**These mismatches do not prove that Table 4 is wrong.** They show that the disclosed candidate models do not reproduce its native calculation. The source values are preserved, with no fitted leakage rate, guessed correction or accepted retention factor. The cited Ha-Duong and Keith (2003) and other valuation originals remain unverified.

The conclusion also literally prints “as small as widely suggested (>0.01)” without an explicit unit. The greater-than sign and missing basis remain unresolved. They are not silently converted to `<0.01%`.

## Credit expiry is distinct from physical retention

The article reports historical maximum crediting periods of 21 years for energy and 60 years for forestry. Its footnote gives nonrenewable alternatives of 10/30 years, or 7/20-year terms with two renewals. These maxima reproduce arithmetically. They are historical credit terms, not retention measurements or current eligibility determinations.

Figure 2 illustrates benefits beyond the crediting period that may not receive credit. Its axes are unscaled, and the 0/1/5/10% schematic lines do not identify a calibrated release law. Replacing expiring credits can be financially equivalent to releasing the surviving stock even while that stock remains underground.

The disclosed finite-expiry examples make the distinction explicit. For **zero physical leakage**, a constant permanent-credit price and replacement at credit expiry, the financial value fraction is `1 − (1+r)^(-T)`:

| Discount rate | 21-year credit | 60-year credit | Physical stock remaining |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1% | 18.86% | 44.96% | 100% |
| 5% | 64.11% | 94.65% | 100% |
| 10% | 86.49% | 99.67% | 100% |

These are analyst cash flows, not the authors' Table 4, observed storage performance or current credit-market prices. They show why financial value cannot be used as a retained-carbon fraction. The increasing-price footnote also needs qualifications: under an annual fractional-release model, unrestricted replacement present value requires `(1−lambda)*(1+g)/(1+r) < 1`. Some sufficiently fast price-growth paths diverge. No native growing-price scenario is claimed reproduced.
