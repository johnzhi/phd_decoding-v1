## Physical retention does not establish local safety

The paper concerns U.S. environmental, health and safety liability. It expressly leaves more detailed climate-regime liability for future work. Its risk discussion includes displaced brine, aquifer or resource contamination, heave and seismic effects **even if CO₂ remains underground**. Those hazards are distinct from atmospheric leakage or lost climate benefit. Qualitative statements about risk declining after injection rely on cited aquifer models; they do not supply a universal pressure-decay law, measured failure distribution or annual injection limit.

The legal discussion is conditional and historical. RCRA waste classifications, CERCLA hazardous-substance and response-cost provisions, mixtures or reaction products, petroleum/useful-product exclusions and commodity arguments depend on governing law and facts. The paper's interpretations do not establish a present exemption, permit or project liability determination. Private response-cost recovery under a statute is also distinct from personal-injury, property, punitive or injunctive remedies under state law.

Table 1 compares trespass, negligence, negligence per se, private nuisance, public nuisance and strict liability across description, elements, tests, examples and CCS application. Regulatory compliance is not automatically freedom from nuisance. Intent to act, breach, causation, protected class, standing and dangerous-activity standards are different questions. ## Monitoring duration is not a transfer certificate

The proposed system has three phases:

| Phase | Responsibility proposed by the authors |
| --- | --- |
| Active injection | Operator liability and insurance; contributions toward a central stewardship fund |
| Postclosure | Operator monitoring, verification, remediation and full damage liability, with financial assurance |
| Long-term stewardship | Public or hybrid responsibility after defined performance conditions and sufficient funding; continued monitoring, remediation, damage finance and data/site management |

The paper discusses 15 or 30 years as examples and 50 years in the then-proposed EPA rules. It explicitly says those proposed rules **“require monitoring but do not have any provisions for a transfer of responsibility.”** A quoted plume-stabilization/no-endangerment condition is suggested as a possible inspiration for a transfer standard; it is not a numerical threshold or implemented certification scheme.

Consequently, elapsed years do not establish physical permanence, zero remaining liability or a duty-free site. Actual performance criteria, transfer effect, party responsibilities and residual recourse would have to be defined. The underlying 2008 proposed rule, subsequent developments and current jurisdiction-specific law remain unverified.

## Financial instruments have different scope and timing

Table 2 compares four mechanisms across five criteria:

- **Bonding:** an upfront commitment, with a defined term, responsible party and cost basis. The amount posted need not cover all damage.
- **Insurance:** classification/pricing, exclusions, deductibles and claims timing matter. The reported belief that EIL insurance can manage environmental risks, possibly excluding climate risk, is not an audited coverage contract. Claims-made and backward-looking descriptions do not establish unlimited future reporting or tail cover.
- **Special fund:** rapid payment may coexist with claims beyond the fund's limit. Proposed per-ton contributions could later depend on monitoring information, but no native charge or calibrated loss model is supplied.
- **Blended insurance/pool:** candidate arrangements can include caps and federal backing. Describing proponents' preemption arguments is not an endorsement of blanket preemption by the authors.

The text calls for preserving statutory/common-law backstops and avoiding blanket state absorption of commercial liability. Its cap discussion covers early pilots and, in some passages, long-term stewardship; the final summary uses narrower pilot wording. Party, phase, funding limit, excess recourse and cap scope need explicit terms before implementation. Neither a fund ceiling nor an operator cap limits physical harm.

The nuclear, Superfund, Trans-Alaska Pipeline and Oil Pollution Act examples are historical analogies. The nuclear figures—300 million dollars of primary insurance, up to 98.5 million per contributing company and approximately 10 billion in aggregate—do not establish a current CCS tariff, contributor/reactor count or immediately available cash. Likewise, 1.6 billion dollars over five years gives an arithmetic average of 320 million/year, not a native CCS funding schedule.

## Disclosed pooling and funding examples

The paper reports a conceptual legal/funding proposal rather than a numerical actuarial or reservoir model. The following analyst examples check implications under explicit assumptions; they do not validate its legal regime or establish calibrated CCS risks.

For two equal sites, assume event probability 0.1 at each site, severity 100 loss units per event and a pool holding 100 units. A mixture parameter moves from independent events to perfectly shared events:

| Dependence mixture | Mean total loss | Variance | Expected loss above available funding |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0, independent | 20 | 1,800 | 1 |
| 0.5 | 20 | 2,700 | 5.5 |
| 1, shared | 20 | 3,600 | 10 |

The expected total loss in the example stays the same. Pooling and funding affect payment availability and who bears a shortfall; they do not themselves remove harm. Geographic diversification therefore needs an actual joint-risk assessment. All probabilities, severities, dependence and fund amounts in this example are analyst choices.

A separate monitoring-only example assumes three end-year deposits based on 1, 2 and 3 million tonnes, a fee of 2 currency units/t and subsequent monitoring costs of 1 million currency units/year. At zero real return, the fund at closure is 12 million. The required charge would be 2.5, 5 or 8.33 units/t for illustrative 15, 30 or 50-year payment horizons. At a 2% real return, these conditional charges are approximately 2.11, 3.68 and 5.17 units/t.

This example begins payments immediately after injection for illustration. It does **not** reproduce the proposal's later transfer or phase-specific expense allocation, and excludes remediation, damage claims, insolvency, inflation and contract terms. The chosen horizons are financial examples, not automatic handoff dates. No actual contribution rate, adequate reserve, insurance premium or physical injection service is inferred.
