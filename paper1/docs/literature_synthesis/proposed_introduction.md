## Introduction

Choosing a geological destination for captured carbon dioxide requires more than comparing transport distance with a reported storage resource. An industrial source needs storage that is available when capture occurs and can accept the supplied flow under well, pressure, thermal and operating constraints. A large cumulative resource can coexist with a deficit during a particular period. Early pinch-analysis work makes this timing problem explicit, while later multiperiod source–sink studies represent commissioning, well development and changing allocations [B3-11, pp3–8; B4-06, pp7–10,12–16]. For domestic and cross-border pathways, the climate question is whether additional retained source CO2 outweighs the additional emissions required to achieve that retention under a common production counterfactual.

Regional assessments identify important opportunities but answer a different question from project deliverability. Zhang and Lau map source and storage candidates around Singapore and estimate cumulative oil, gas and aquifer resources. Chinese integrated screening combines resource tiers, suitability, economics and cross-regional pairing [B4-08, pp2–10,15–18; B2-14, pp3–9]. Pressure-aware global assessments further show that pore-space estimates alone are insufficient. Their results nevertheless depend on development assumptions, spatial aggregation, boundaries and the injection horizon [B2-15, pp2–5,10–12; B2-18, pp10–12]. These studies support regional screening and prioritisation; they do not allocate a named industrial source a verified annual storage service or establish current legal and commercial access.

The economic interaction between transport and storage is already established. Wang et al. show that distance, injectivity and cumulative capacity jointly influence pipeline and sink selection. Nordic comparisons similarly find circumstances in which transport to a more distant, more injective destination can be financially attractive [B2-01, pp2–9; B3-33, pp3,8,14; B3-39, p1]. Such a result is not automatically a climate preference. Extra capture, conditioning, vessel fuel, wells, water handling and construction can change the balance, while an economic objective may include revenues or credits with no equivalent physical removal of greenhouse gases. Route length and cost alone therefore cannot resolve the difference in net mitigation.

Temporal and spatial coordination are also established modelling capabilities. SimCCS jointly represents source and reservoir opening, pipeline sizing and network flows, although its original formulation is atemporal. Subsequent work combines multiperiod capture retrofits, source–sink limits and compensatory generation, and compares yearly domestic and overseas rollout against a business-as-usual reference [B3-44, pp2–8; B3-14, pp3,6–7; B3-42, pp1–7,15–17]. Stochastic deployment models add commissioning budgets and uncertain storage volumes; risk-aware models distinguish capacity hedges from shortfall penalties [B2-03, pp3–7; B2-09, pp6–17]. Dynamic planning, uncertain capacity and a common energy-service baseline are consequently relevant precedents rather than sufficient novelty claims for another network model.

Recent studies bring additional physical and transport detail into infrastructure decisions. Bertoni et al. embed reduced reservoir dynamics in a mixed-integer optimisation framework. Budiarto et al. connect well-derived injection limits with multiperiod matching in South Sumatra [B2-04, pp5–11; B4-06, pp7–16]. Burger et al. represent limited pipeline expansion and temporary multimodal alternatives, while Ayotte-Sauvé et al. include phase changes, buffers, fleets and offshore interfaces [B2-05, pp3–10; B2-06, pp5–13]. These developments show why a comparison must specify which physical constraints, time scales and decisions are actually represented. Accuracy within a reduced model's tested domain, an annual fleet constraint and a published allocation schedule provide different evidence from a calibrated reservoir limit or a feasible sequence of ship movements.

Operating and monitoring studies explain what is needed to connect those representations to physical service. Ketzin and Decatur investigations combine pressure at several depths with arrival, saturation and other observations; they also reveal parameter correlation, measurement-reference issues and differences between a successful history match and an independent forecast [B1-14, pp3–8; B1-15, pp4–8; B1-16, pp2–11; B1-18, pp3–10]. Ketzin operating guidance demonstrates the importance of distinguishing wellhead and downhole pressure, gauge and absolute values, and single- from two-phase behaviour [B1-17, pp3–10]. Malaysian studies add case-specific cooling, completion, restart, fault and brine-management constraints [B1-06, pp3–7; B1-08, pp4–12; B1-11, pp5–6; B1-13, pp8–13]. These mechanisms justify explicit feasibility checks, but do not support converting a published pressure change or failure map into a universal annual derating or retention factor.

Shipping introduces a second set of coupled time and state constraints. A fleet with sufficient aggregate annual capacity may still fail to maintain capture if departures, terminal service, buffer space and storage acceptance do not align. Stochastic shipping studies already link buffers, sailing conditions and operating responses; their results depend on completion targets and information available when decisions are made [B2-08, pp3–8]. Low-pressure ship/medium-pressure terminal studies show that vapour return, excess vapour, heating and electrical work require separate accounting [B1-20, pp3–14]. BOG process studies likewise distinguish recovery expenditure from purge and generator emissions, while dynamic tank/cargo-handling work already connects operating strategies to greenhouse-gas intensity [B1-19, pp6–13; B3-29, pp4–10]. Generated BOG, recovered liquid, retained heel and atmospheric venting must therefore remain separate mass states.

Climate comparisons depend just as strongly on functional units and system boundaries. Onboard-capture results can rely on recovered exhaust heat and LNG cold, and may report demand per tonne avoided rather than per tonne captured. CHP capture can require steam extraction, replacement electricity or heat-pump work whose consequences depend on the reference heating and power system [B1-01, pp6–9; B2-11, pp3–5,10]. Prospective assessment adds changing background energy systems and technology maturity [B2-02, pp5–21]. Postcapture cost/GHG optimisation and prospective transport LCA already examine alternative chains and carbon payback, but neither supplies a complete matched industrial capture-to-retention inventory for a new region [B2-12, pp6–9,18–21; B2-13, pp2–10]. Captured, transported, injected, stored and avoided CO2 consequently require explicit denominators and compatible utility boundaries.

These precedents motivate a controlled model-comparison question: under the same source and production counterfactual, when does resolving annual storage service and operational shipping change the feasibility, magnitude or sign of the domestic-versus-foreign comparison? The analysis should distinguish changing the source mix from changing its destination, and distinguish a commissioning delay from a post-commissioning rate constraint. It should also evaluate decisions chosen by simpler representations under a common detailed model. Proxy-to-simulator reevaluation is itself established in well-placement research, and reservoir studies provide examples in which substantial rate variation has limited long-horizon effects [B3-23, pp8,12; B3-37, pp3–7]. An unchanged ranking, small interaction or infeasible selected decision is an informative outcome.

Paper 1 addresses this question through a source-traceable framework and controlled benchmarks. It separates static and annual storage-service representations from fixed-factor and operational shipping representations, preserves common-source controls, and reevaluates selected plans with explicit inventories and scheduling conventions. Published pressure, conditioning, buffer, process and fleet results are retained only within their supported boundaries. The comparison reports the remaining allowance for omitted differential burdens where complete lifecycle inputs are unavailable, rather than presenting that partial result as full net avoided emissions. The intended contribution is the transparent test of how representations and evidence limitations affect a decision; regional geological calibration, full lifecycle comparison and present storage access remain separate requirements.
