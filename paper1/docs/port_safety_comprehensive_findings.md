## Three distinct recommendations

The article reviews UK port handling and temporary storage of CO2. Its central proposals concern different instruments and mechanisms:

| Instrument | Authors' finding and proposed response | Audit qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Port Marine Safety Code (PMSC) | Performance-based safety management needs clear goals in underlying legislation and suitable guidance. | Voluntary Code compliance does not remove applicable statutory duties under general health and safety legislation. No site-specific MSMS or numerical ALARP assessment is performed. |
| Dangerous Goods in Harbour Areas Regulations 2016 (DGHAR) | Harbour masters already have relevant powers; bespoke MCA guidance could improve recognition and management of growing CO2 traffic. | Powers depend on actor, location, danger criteria, exceptions and local instruments. Proposed guidance is not an observed new publication or project permission. |
| Control of Major Accident Hazards Regulations 2015 (COMAH) | Address both the transport/directly related temporary-storage exclusion and CO2's absence from the relevant trigger classification. | These are independent barriers in the authors' account. Recognition alone does not remove the separate exclusion. Exact statutory exceptions, tiers and current applicability remain unverified. |

For COMAH recognition the authors discuss three alternatives: explicit Part 2 naming, classification within a qualifying Part 1 hazard category, or Part 3 paragraph 5 equivalent major-accident potential. Table 2 assesses their institutional feasibility and dependencies. Its word **operability** does not describe a ship/terminal pressure-temperature envelope. The equivalence route's possible avoidance of a recognition amendment does not itself resolve the transport/storage exclusion.

These are **2025 authors' recommendations, not verified adopted 2026 requirements**. General H&S law remains relevant. The paper is not a complete maritime compliance inventory: other ship-to-ship and ship-shore standards are expressly outside its principal analysis. UK findings do not automatically apply to Asian ports.

## Hazard, classification, quantity and exposure differ

The paper distinguishes nonflammability from the toxic/asphyxiation consequences of high CO2 concentrations, dense-cloud accumulation in low or confined spaces, and pressurized cryogenic handling. IMDG Class 2.2 terminology does not establish that a large release is physically harmless.

Pressure/cryogenic warnings H280/H281, a substance's CLP classification, a COMAH trigger, inventory thresholds, actual exposure and risk are different questions. The 10/50/5 tonne examples for other gases are not CO2 limits. Table 1's **Yes** for CO2 equivalence records the authors' reasoning; no official designation, original toxicology experiment, LC50/ATE calculation, exposure simulation or calibrated accident frequency is supplied. Quantity alone cannot establish an intrinsic acute-toxicity classification.

The cited 2008 fire-extinguishing incident reportedly released 15 tonnes, intoxicated 107 people, hospitalized 16 and involved one person requiring intensive care. The source wording leaves the ICU person's subset/additional status unresolved. The audit does not sum this into 17 admissions or infer casualties per tonne. The bibliography identifies a 2013 report accessed in 2024; the access year is not the accident or report publication year.

The generic −20 to −50°C and 10–25 bar descriptions are unpaired ranges. Gauge/absolute pressure, composition and matched states are unknown. They therefore do not establish a rectangular liquid operating envelope, actual density, phase margin or utility duty.

The 40.5 Mt/11% Q1 2024 comparison remains quarterly; it is not multiplied into an observed annual quantity.

The CCSA scenario combines 15 Mt/y domestic capture and 20 Mt/y imports by 2035. Note 2 specifies the **Enabling Industry Pipeline** scenario rather than constrained technology-specific targets. The physical scenario total is 35 Mt/y. Comparing it with 17.95 Mt of all liquid gases in 2023 combines different dates, commodities and potentially handling boundaries. It establishes neither observed CO2 growth nor annual injection service.

A conditional analyst boundary example keeps physical CO2 at 35 Mt/y: if domestic tonnes each count once at a UK port and imported tonnes once, handling is 35 Mt/y; if domestic port-to-port loads and unloads each count separately, it becomes 50 Mt/y. **This is not a verified DfT counting convention or a finding that the authors double counted.** It records a definition that must be checked in the original statistics before such comparisons become model inputs.

The cited Solent case uses 5.9 Mt/y, carriers of 7500–32000 m3 and buffers of one-to-two cargoes. With explicitly analyst density 1000 kg/m3, full usable volume and uniform 365-day throughput, endpoint combinations imply 7500–64000 tonnes or approximately 0.464–3.959 equivalent throughput days. These are four illustrative combinations of ranges, not four native optimized designs. Filling fraction, actual density, reserve, berth and vessel schedules, weather and maintenance are unknown. Equivalent capacity days are not actual dwell or proof of adequate service.

Similarly, the source's at-least-24-hour/no-earlier-than-six-month DGHAR notice window is an advance notification requirement with scope and exceptions. It can overlap a voyage. It is not a compulsory 24-hour terminal wait; six calendar months are not silently converted into 180 days.
