## Equations and temporal conventions

Equations 1–7 minimize discounted costs with capacity lifetimes, bounds, a soft stored-CO2 target and offset-linear pipeline capital costs. With consecutive planning indices and a three-year interval, Equation 2 maps nine controls from 2026 through 2050 onto eight three-year cash-flow blocks plus the final year: 25 annual weights. An independent annual cash-stream sum verifies the discount component. Calendar-year differences inserted directly into the same exponent would count the interval twice; the native index convention must be checked in code.

Equation 3 uses a ceiling to retain capacity in whole planning blocks. In an analyst example, a ten-year asset remains for four three-year blocks. This is a discretization qualification, not a claim about the undisclosed native technology lifetimes. Existing capacity, construction leads, terminal treatment and salvage require the complete model.

The soft target in Equation 6 is meaningful only with the native penalty calibration. An independent analyst LP with a feasible unit of service costing six chooses slack when the penalty is five, and service when it is seven. Thus a finite penalty does not itself guarantee slack occurs only under infeasibility. The main PDF does not disclose the actual penalty; this example establishes no failure of the authors’ implementation.

Equations 8–14 limit additions using historical knowledge and pioneering projects, either on individual edges or across regional edge clusters. Capacity, distance and their product are different metrics. Distance is a fixed coefficient, so its product with a capacity decision remains linear. Cluster definitions, initial knowledge, installation/upgrade binary semantics and the temporal units of pioneering additions need the native implementation. Rated annual flow, tonnes transported and annual construction capability must remain distinct.

With zero depreciation, knowledge survives the retirement of a physical asset. That distinction is intentional and need not violate a material balance. Pooling also allows experience from one edge to support another within the same cluster. The [nine-row example](../outputs/comprehensive_review/B2-05_diffusion_example.csv) independently implements the printed reinforcing bound, without demand, network or construction constraints. A separate linear-system calculation verifies it. Such bounds do not contain an intrinsic logistic saturation; finite demand and other model constraints can still limit actual deployment. Historical S-shaped diffusion and project-success analogies do not establish a calibrated risk distribution for this case.

## Results that remain qualified

The body reports a **4.8 Mt** cumulative shortfall, approximately 9% of the 53 Mt target. Figure 3’s caption instead reports **1.7 Mt**, approximately 3.2%. Figure 4 describes a sum of penalty variables without showing interval weights. If those variables are annual rates, three-year blocks require integration. If they are already period masses, a simple sum is appropriate.

One analyst example has annual-rate gaps of 0.70, 0.85 and 0.15: their simple sum is 1.7, while weights of 3, 3 and 1 give 4.8 Mt. **This is only a possible mathematical explanation.** It is not a recovered author series, digitization or resolution of the conflict. Exact annual targets, gaps and native integration conventions remain necessary. The paper’s “nine years” description of 2041–2050 also needs its elapsed-time versus annual-block convention preserved.

The reported 200 kt lifecycle emissions from alternative transport equal 4.17% of the body’s 4.8 Mt gap, supporting the stated “less than 5%” arithmetic under that denominator. Using the conflicting caption value would give 11.76%. Neither calculation is a complete incremental lifecycle comparison of the two systems.

The scenario descriptions impose a 60% minimum pipeline utilization in scenarios 2–4, while the unrestricted baseline has very low early utilization. Thus the scenarios differ in this stated restriction as well as in deployment constraints or available modes. Native activity/shutoff conventions and a controlled comparison are needed to isolate effects. A connected Delaunay graph likewise does not prove that excluding offshore edges longer than 500 km has a small effect on cost or feasible flow.

The 2–13% barge shares are tonne-kilometre shares. The figures’ capacity-distance bars and reported 8%/40% system changes must not be converted into source mass fractions, vessel counts, operating calendars or calibrated injection capacity. Figure 3b is the pipeline-only scenario; its citation in the scenario 3 discussion is an incorrect pointer to the multimodal result shown in Figure 5a. Exact graphic arrays were not inferred from pixels.
